These are the questions I would once have typed into a search engine and then given up on after the third ad.

## Is this term or whole life insurance?

I had a document about life insurance through work and could not tell what kind it was. I uploaded it and asked.

The answer was group term life, and the useful part was why:

- Coverage ends when you leave the job.
- The benefit goes down as you get older.
- There is an option to convert it, with a 30-day window.
- There is no cash value.

It then told me to ask HR or the insurer for the certificate of coverage if I wanted it confirmed officially. This is information, not financial advice.

## Can I fix this bumper myself?

Someone hit my car in a parking lot and left. I wanted the cheapest repair I could do myself.

Claude's first guess was wrong. It thought the black mark was paint from the other car and suggested polishing it off. I told it the black was the bare plastic of the bumper showing through.

That changed the whole answer. The new one was a proper sequence: clean, sand, a special primer that makes paint stick to plastic, color, then clear coat. It included how to spray metallic paint so the flakes lie flat, and a warning about weather.

The lesson is that it could not see my car. I could. When I corrected it, the advice got good.

## What does "three dots and a dash" mean?

I saw the phrase and asked. Three dots and a dash is Morse code for the letter V, which stood for victory in the Second World War.

Then I mentioned I had seen it as the name of a place in Chicago. It is a tiki bar, named for a cocktail invented to celebrate the end of the war. The drink's garnish is three cherries and a piece of pineapple: three dots and a dash.

## What I take from this

You can ask a follow-up. That is the whole difference from a search engine. The second question is usually where the good answer is.

## Where it falls short

It guesses when it cannot see. Describe the thing carefully, and correct it when it is wrong.
